Dear Editor,

Please consider our manuscript, "Coordinating biochar and geological storage for biomass based carbon dioxide removal," for publication in *Energy & Environmental Science* as a Full Paper.

Crop residues are the largest biomass resource for carbon dioxide removal (CDR) that requires no additional land, but the same residue can be used only once. Mitigation scenarios almost always assign it to bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), yet residues can also be pyrolyzed to biochar and returned to soil (pyrogenic carbon capture and storage, PyCCS), or combusted for electricity alone. No previous study has compared these three uses for the same residue, at the resolution at which soils, crops, electricity prices, and distance to geological storage vary. Our manuscript does this at 0.1–0.2° resolution across the United States, Europe, China, and India, with CO~2~ routed to characterized storage basins and biochar valued through its effects on crop yield, soil acidity, nutrient supply, and N~2~O emissions.

The main findings are:

- The technologies do not rank in a fixed order. PyCCS is the least-cost removal at moderate carbon prices because its agronomic value pays for part of the removal; in China and India it is profitable without any carbon price on residue that abates about 110 Tg CO~2~e per year. BECCS stores 2.2–2.5 times more carbon per megagram of residue and is preferred above regional median crossover prices of 130–210 US$ per Mg CO~2~e. Bioenergy without capture is rarely the best use of residue at any carbon price.
- Abatement from the four regions rises from about 650 Tg CO~2~e per year at 100 US$ per Mg CO~2~e to about 2,030 Tg at 250 US$ per Mg CO~2~e, with the second step coming from the move to geological storage.
- The crossover price varies within and between regions with crop value, soil cation exchange capacity, feedstock cost, electricity price, and distance to storage. A single global supply curve for residue-based CDR would misstate both the price at which each technology is preferred and where.
- Along a carbon-price trajectory consistent with limiting warming to 2 °C, BECCS outcompetes PyCCS within the operating life of PyCCS plants built in the early 2030s. Where shared CO~2~ transport and storage are available, building BECCS directly obtains about 11 Gt CO~2~e more by 2069 than building PyCCS first; where they are not, PyCCS has the higher net present value and is the low-regret first step.

These results matter for how integrated assessment models and national strategies represent biomass CDR: both technologies have a place, in different regions and at different carbon prices, and planning the transition between them achieves more CDR at lower cost than relying on BECCS alone. We believe the work suits *Energy & Environmental Science* because it combines energy-system economics, process engineering, and biogeochemistry to address a question that each field has so far treated in isolation, and because its conclusions bear directly on energy and climate policy.
