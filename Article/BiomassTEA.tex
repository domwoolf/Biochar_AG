\title{Optimal biomass-based climate change mitigation pathways are
spatially heterogeneous due to localized technoeconomic and biophysical
constraints}
\author{}
\date{2026-10-05}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
The use of biomass for climate change mitigation offers substantial
technical potential, but optimal deployment is highly dependent on
spatial, technoeconomic, and biophysical constraints. Blanket climate
policies frequently fail to capture this heterogeneity. Here we apply a
spatially explicit techno-economic and consequential life cycle
assessment framework deployed at a 0.1-degree resolution across the
United States, Europe, India, and China. We evaluate the economic
viability and carbon abatement potential of three distinct biomass
utilization pathways: Bioenergy (BES), Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and
Storage (BECCS), and Bioenergy with Biochar Carbon Storage (BEBCS).
Localized constraints, such as distance to geological
CO\textsubscript{2} sinks, baseline grid carbon intensity, and native
soil cation exchange capacity, dictate the economically optimal pathway.
Traditional BES tends to dominate at low carbon prices, particularly in
regions with high wholesale energy prices like Europe, or high grid
carbon intensities like India. Biochar (BEBCS) emerges as a highly
competitive transition technology at moderate carbon prices, and serves
as the optimal long-term pathway in specific agro-ecological zones with
degraded soils, such as southern China. Conversely, high capital costs
and piecewise long-distance pipeline penalties act as severe barriers to
BECCS, which only achieves universal dominance at high carbon prices
(e.g., 150 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e) where
carbon capture revenues finally overwhelm infrastructure penalties.
Ultimately, effective climate mitigation requires localized policies
that match biomass resources to their most efficient regional end-use.
\end{abstract}


\section{INTRODUCTION}\label{introduction}

Biochar has been widely promoted for climate change mitigation, with
co-benefits for soil health and crop production when used as a soil
amendment.(\citeproc{ref-woolf2010}{\emph{1}},
\citeproc{ref-lehmann2021biochar}{\emph{2}}) Globally, biochar has the
sustainable technical potential for carbon sequestration and emissions
reductions of up to 3.4--6.3 Pg CO\textsubscript{2}e. However, the
costs, benefits and tradeoffs for biochar vary spatially, creating
substantial heterogeneity in its feasibility at a given carbon price.
This variability is further affected by the fact that biochar must
compete with alternative uses for the limited supply of available waste
biomass. Indeed, alternative uses of biomass, such as producing
bioenergy with or without carbon capture and sequestration can also help
to mitigate climate change and may provide greater climate mitigation,
lower costs, and/or greater co-benefits in certain
contexts.(\citeproc{ref-woolf2016}{\emph{3}}) This depends on a number
of different factors, including soil type, cropland productivity,
climate, energy fuel mix, feedstock availability, distance to
CO\textsubscript{2} sinks for carbon capture and storage, energy prices
and food prices. For example, bioenergy production may provide a greater
climate benefit in regions that are highly dependent on carbon-intensive
energy sources and have high soil fertility, whereas biochar is more
favorable where low-fertility soils can benefit from its soil-health
improvement.(\citeproc{ref-woolf2016}{\emph{3}})

It is, therefore, critical to understand where biochar could be the best
use of resources from a climate mitigation perspective. Currently, most
biochar assessments focus on the technical potential, without mapping
out the tradeoffs or identifying optimal pathways based on location.
While there are some biochar life-cycle assessments that account for
alternative biomass uses, they are highly context-specific and difficult
to scale up on(\citeproc{ref-peters2015}{\emph{4}},
\citeproc{ref-lefebvre2021}{\emph{5}}). As such, this project aims to
provide a detailed feasibility and tradeoff analysis across four regions
(North America, India, China, and Europe) to map where biochar
represents the most efficient use of biomass to reduce atmospheric
greenhouse gasses (GHGs). These regions represent a diversity of
geographic, socio-economic, and agro-ecological conditions for a broad
assessment of how constraints, tradeoffs and benefits vary across
regions. This work will allow policy-makers and practitioners to make
scientifically-informed decisions about which mitigation pathways are
best suited for their geographies. It can also help contextualize
biochar's mitigation potential by identifying areas that maximize its
climate benefits and realistically support its production and
application.

\section{RESULTS}\label{results}

\subsection{Capital-intensive pathways are highly sensitive to discount
rates and carbon
prices}\label{capital-intensive-pathways-are-highly-sensitive-to-discount-rates-and-carbon-prices}

Figure~\ref{fig-evap-usa} to Figure~\ref{fig-evap-china} illustrate the
core vulnerability of CDR pathways to capital lock-out. Because both
BECCS (heavy upfront infrastructure) and BEBCS (deferred agronomic
value) are highly sensitive to the cost of capital, high discount rates
act as a strong barrier to adoption, forcing a retreat to traditional
Bioenergy (BES) without high carbon prices. For example, in the USA, at
a 2\% social discount rate and a 30 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e carbon price, BEBCS covers a large extent of the US
Midwest and South, with BECCS only being viable close to
CO\textsubscript{2} sinks. However, stepping up to the 8\% market
baseline eliminates BEBCS's advantage, replacing it with almost entirely
with BES. The value of biochar relies heavily on liming and nutrient
substitution benefits that are amortized over a 10-year agronomic impact
duration. At an 8\% or 15\% discount rate, the present value of those
future soil benefits is diminished, rendering the upfront thermal
penalties of pyrolysis economically unviable at low carbon prices. At a
2\% discount rate and intermediate carbon price (around 100 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e), BECCS is nearly universal
across the US. At 15\%, the BECCS dominance is fractured, with BES
reclaiming the geographic margins (e.g., the Northeast and far West),
and BEBCS remaining only in localized pockets where soil conditions make
it particularly attractive. This occurs because, when capital is
expensive, the penalty for long-distance pipelines becomes
insurmountable until carbon prices reach the 150 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e range. By the time the
carbon price reaches 150 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e, the maps become almost entirely red across all
discount rates. At this pricing level, the high CDR capture efficiency
overwhelms the high cost of capital, making BECCS the most economically
attractive option.

\subsection{Biochar provides a mid-price transition technology option
and a long-term solution for specific agro-ecological
zones}\label{biochar-provides-a-mid-price-transition-technology-option-and-a-long-term-solution-for-specific-agro-ecological-zones}

Figure~\ref{fig-evap-china} provides a counterpoint to the US results.
It shows how soils with strong production constraints that can be
alleviated by biochar can create a persistent competitive advantage for
biochar, particularly when combined with large distances to
CO\textsubscript{2} storage sites. BEBCS performs a central role as a
transition technology that peaks at intermediate carbon prices that are
high enough to make its carbon sequestration competitive with BES, but
not so high that the more efficient CDR of BECCS per unit biomass
outcompetes it (Figure~\ref{fig-macc}). Nonetheless, while BECCS
ultimately becomes the optimal technology across the entire US spatial
domain at high carbon prices, BEBCS remains the most viable pathway in
southern China across almost all price and discount rate scenarios. This
demonstrates that BEBCS is not merely a transition technology, but the
optimal long-term pathway for specific agro-ecological zones. In
Southern China, characterized by highly weathered, acidic soils, the
biochar's liming and nutrient use efficiency benefits provide a
substantial economic credit that buffers against high processing costs.
The model scales physical yield efficiency gains inversely to the soil's
native Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC), thus granting severe CEC deficits
higher premiums. This is confirmed by the SHAP analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig-shap-dominant}), which identifies soil CEC as the
dominant spatial driver in this region.

\subsection{Regional policies hinder biochar
competitiveness}\label{regional-policies-hinder-biochar-competitiveness}

In India, on the other hand, the mid carbon price advantage of BEBCS is
almost entirely eliminated, with BECCS eventually covering most of the
map at high carbon prices. Here, fertilizer subsidies hamper BEBCS's
economic viability. Because BEBCS economics relies on improved nutrient
use efficiency and lower fertilizer inputs per unit crop yield, cheap
subsidized synthetic fertilizers erode biochar's nutrient substitution
value. Despite this, Biochar maintains a strong foothold in the regions
in the North East, North West, and central Eastern coastal regions of
India where low soil CEC is a major constraint to crop production.

\subsection{Regional energy prices and carbon intensity provide a
critical constraint on the optimal CDR
pathways}\label{regional-energy-prices-and-carbon-intensity-provide-a-critical-constraint-on-the-optimal-cdr-pathways}

At lower carbon prices The optimal technology in Europe is almost
universally BES, driven by high energy prices as the dominant driver
across Europe. At 30 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e,
the revenue from maximized power generation in BES outweighs the
incentive to capture and sequester carbon, rendering the capital
expenditure for BECCS and BEBCS unviable across all discount rates. As
carbon prices increase, BECCS begins to dominate across central and
northern Europe. BEBCS gains a strong advantage in Southern Europe, and
Eastern Scandinavia where various combinations of large distances to
CO\textsubscript{2} sinks, already-low carbon intensity of the energy
systems, and infertile soils make it more competitive. As in China, we
see BEBCS maintain its advantage in these regions, even at very high
carbon prices.

The value of bioenergy power also depends on how it is dispatched within
the wider power system, which this plant-level analysis represents only
approximately. Each plant here processes a fixed annual quantity of
biomass, set by its capacity and capacity factor, and BES chooses
between base-load and flexible operation on its own net value. In system
models that optimise capacity and hourly dispatch together, biomass is a
scarce, storable fuel that the system directs to the hours and uses
where it is most valuable, such as periods of high residual load, so
that its operating regime depends on the generation mix, demand and
other sources of flexibility of each grid
(\citeproc{ref-johansson2019}{\emph{6}}--\citeproc{ref-ahlstrom2022}{\emph{8}}).
Where such flexibility is scarce, the value of BES, and of BECCS
operated below base load, may therefore be higher than estimated here;
where it is abundant, base-load operation, as assumed here, is closer to
the system optimum.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Plant size and CO\textsubscript{2}
infrastructure shift the technology
mix}{Plant size and CO2 infrastructure shift the technology mix}}\label{plant-size-and-co2-infrastructure-shift-the-technology-mix}

Three structural assumptions that the Monte Carlo analysis holds fixed
were tested by repeating the carbon-price sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig-si-variants}). Plant size has the largest effect.
Larger plants favour the more capital-intensive technologies through
economies of scale: with 250 MW\textsubscript{th} instead of 125
MW\textsubscript{th} plants, BECCS is adopted at lower carbon prices,
and in the United States abatement at 150 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e rises from 46 to 337 Tg CO\textsubscript{2}e
yr\textsuperscript{-1}. With 50 MW\textsubscript{th} plants, adoption is
delayed and BEBCS keeps a larger share: in the United States at 200 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e, BEBCS provides 243 of 257
Tg CO\textsubscript{2}e yr\textsuperscript{-1}, against 91 of 529 Tg
CO\textsubscript{2}e yr\textsuperscript{-1} by default. In Europe, 250
MW\textsubscript{th} BES plants are profitable even without a carbon
price. Where almost all biomass is already used at high carbon prices,
as in China and India above about 150 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e, plant size changes the timing of adoption but not
the eventual abatement. These results should be read alongside the
constraints on plant size that the model does not represent (see
Methods): economies of scale favour ever larger plants, but road
traffic, nuisance and local infrastructure limit the size of biomass
plants in agricultural landscapes. Requiring each plant to build a
dedicated pipeline to its sink, rather than share trunklines, delays
BECCS and shifts biomass to BEBCS: at 150 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e, total abatement falls from 1,124 to 885 Tg
CO\textsubscript{2}e yr\textsuperscript{-1} in China and from 287 to 209
Tg CO\textsubscript{2}e yr\textsuperscript{-1} in Europe. Allowing
CO\textsubscript{2}-EOR as a sink raises abatement at intermediate
carbon prices in China (from 731 to 821 Tg CO\textsubscript{2}e
yr\textsuperscript{-1} at 100 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e) but by 5\% or less in the other regions.

\subsection{Competing uses of crop
residues}\label{competing-uses-of-crop-residues}

The analysis values crop residues against two counterfactuals: retention
in the field, whose net soil GHG effect is close to zero
(\citeproc{ref-mcclelland2025}{\emph{9}}), and open burning, whose
avoided CH\textsubscript{4} and N\textsubscript{2}O emissions are
credited. A third use, livestock fodder and bedding, is excluded from
the supply: the sustainably harvestable residue of
(\citeproc{ref-karan2023}{\emph{10}}), which is net of the fraction that
must be retained to avoid soil degradation, is reduced by the fraction
already used as fodder and bedding
(\citeproc{ref-herrero2013}{\emph{11}}). This matters most in India,
where cereal straws are a major source of fodder, whereas rice straw in
the north-western rice--wheat belt is largely burned. Where the fodder
fraction is underestimated locally, diverting residues would displace
feed that must be replaced from other sources, with associated
emissions, land use and costs; the net abatement estimated here is
therefore most robust where residues would otherwise be burned or left
in the field.

\section{DISCUSSION}\label{discussion}

The deployment of biomass for climate change mitigation cannot be guided
by a single universal pathway. Our spatial analysis demonstrates that
the economic viability of BES, BECCS, and BEBCS is dictated by a complex
interplay of localized biophysical constraints and regional market
dynamics.

While BECCS offers the highest theoretical carbon removal potential, its
heavy infrastructure requirements and sensitivity to the cost of capital
create significant barriers to entry, particularly where biomass is
located far from suitable geological sinks. In contrast, biochar (BEBCS)
circumvents these transport constraints and establishes a resilient
economic niche in regions with degraded, low-CEC soils, where its
agronomic co-benefits---such as liming equivalence and nutrient
substitution---offset its lower electrical conversion efficiencies.
Southern China exemplifies this dynamic, demonstrating that BEBCS is a
robust, long-term mitigation strategy for specific agro-ecological zones
rather than merely a transition technology.

Furthermore, baseline energy markets shape optimal pathways. In Europe,
high wholesale electricity prices establish BES as the dominant pathway
at low carbon prices.

Biochar plants could also sell the heat from their surplus pyrolysis
vapours and gases rather than generating electricity. In an exploratory
scenario in which BEBCS sold all its heat to a year-round user, heat
recovery raised the net value of BEBCS in all four regions, because heat
earns more than electricity from the same fuel at a much lower capital
cost, while abating slightly less, as heat displaces less
CO\textsubscript{2} per unit than electricity. Year-round heat demand,
such as an industrial process or a district heating network, is uncommon
in residue-producing regions, so heat recovery is an opportunity for
co-location at particular sites rather than a basis for regional
deployment, and it is not included in the results.

Realizing the climate mitigation potential of global biomass requires
moving beyond blanket technological mandates. Policymakers must adopt
spatially explicit strategies that target BECCS deployment near
geological sinks, incentivize BEBCS in agro-ecological zones suffering
from soil degradation, and recognize the enduring role of BES in
displacing highly carbon-intensive fossil grids.

\section{METHODS}\label{methods}

\subsection{Objectives and design}\label{objectives-and-design}

The C-SCAPE (Carbon Spatial Cost and Allocation Pathways Evaluator)
model (version 1.0; implemented as the R package BiocharAG) evaluates
three biomass utilization pathways: Bioenergy (BES), Bioenergy with
Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS), and Bioenergy with Biochar Carbon
Storage (BEBCS). For each grid cell and technology, the model calculates
a discounted cost-benefit balance, expressed as a levelized annual net
value per megagram of dry, ash-free feedstock (the net present value
annualized with the capital recovery factor; referred to as NPV below).
GHG emissions and removals are converted to 100-year CO\textsubscript{2}
equivalents and internalized using an assumed carbon price.

The model was deployed across the coterminous United States, Europe,
India, and mainland China. Each region is restricted to its national
territory, from the World Bank official boundaries; for China this
excludes Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau, for which regional price data were
not compiled. Europe comprises the countries of geographic Europe within
11°W--40°E and 35--71°N, including Ukraine, Belarus and the part of
Türkiye within these bounds, but excluding Russia. All spatial inputs
were harmonized to a geographic (WGS84) grid of 0.1° (0.2° for the
United States). Area-dependent calculations use cell areas on the
ellipsoid, and the biomass collection distances were computed in
region-specific equal-area projections (EPSG:5070, EPSG:3035 and Albers
equal-area projections for China and India) before being returned to the
model grid. Maps omit islands smaller than 2,500 km\textsuperscript{2},
which are too small to show at figure scale; they are included in all
results. The model was implemented in R, using the terra package for
spatial analysis (\citeproc{ref-hijmans2025terra}{\emph{12}}).

The following model description outlines the assumptions, equations and
parameters used in the model. The default parameter values are provided
in a table at the end of the Methods.

\subsection{Biomass feedstock sourcing and
logistics}\label{biomass-feedstock-sourcing-and-logistics}

\subsubsection{Biomass transport costs and
emissions}\label{biomass-transport-costs-and-emissions}

High resolution rasters of available crop residue biomass were derived
from the sustainably harvestable crop residues of Karan et al.
(\citeproc{ref-karan2023}{\emph{10}}), which exclude the fraction that
must be retained to avoid soil degradation, minus the fraction used as
livestock fodder and bedding, estimated for each world region from the
stover feed use of (\citeproc{ref-herrero2013}{\emph{11}}). The supply
modelled here therefore does not divert residues from these uses. The
average transport distance for biomass was calculated from this using
focal statistics based on the annual biomass requirement for the
conversion facility. The required annual biomass mass (\(M_{req}\)) is
determined by the target thermal capacity, the capacity factor, and the
biomass lower heating value (LHV). Collection distances were computed
for a reference capacity factor of 0.70 and a series of plant sizes;
each technology uses the distance for the plant size with the same
annual biomass demand at its own capacity factor, interpolated in the
square root of plant size. A spatial focal sum algorithm was applied to
aggregate the surrounding biomass over a discrete series of expanding
radii (from 5 km up to 500 km). The required collection radius was then
calculated via quadratic interpolation between these radial bins. All
domains were uniformly projected and resampled to the 0.1 degree
(\(\sim 10\text{ km}\)) equal-area grid prior to executing the focal sum
algorithms to ensure methodological consistency. Assuming a circular
catchment geometry, the average straight-line collection distance is
approximated as two-thirds of the interpolated bounding radius
(i.e.~assuming homogeneous distribution within the catchment area). The
average straight-line distance was multiplied by a tortuosity factor of
1.3 to calculate the effective road transport
distance.(\citeproc{ref-sultana2014}{\emph{13}}) Biomass logistical
costs were modeled using a fixed material handling and loading cost and
a variable trucking cost applied to the effective road distance. The
loading and handling cost is 6.27 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} (4.59 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} dry matter in 2012 USD, escalated to 2024 USD
with the US consumer price index) (\citeproc{ref-beach2012}{\emph{14}}).
The variable cost of 0.19 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
km\textsuperscript{-1} (0.12 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
km\textsuperscript{-1} for straw and stover in 2006 USD, escalated to
2024 USD with the US consumer price index) is the truck
distance-variable cost of (\citeproc{ref-searcy2007}{\emph{15}}), which
is expressed per kilometre of one-way distance and includes the empty
return trip. Each grid cell contributes only its own biomass to the
regional totals, so a grid cell represents a share of a plant rather
than a plant site: the number of plants of each technology follows from
the biomass allocated to it, and its collection distance assumes that
the biomass within the collection radius is not used by a different
technology. Where technologies are intermixed, plants of each technology
keep their design size and draw on interlocking catchments, so their
collection radius increases by about \(1/\sqrt{f}\), where \(f\) is the
share of the biomass within the radius that is not allocated to another
technology. Enlarging the catchment is much cheaper than building
smaller plants, because haulage costs are small relative to the
economies of scale in capital costs. Because the preferred technology is
spatially coherent at the scale of a catchment, \(f\) averages
0.89--1.00 across the carbon prices and discount rates of
Figure~\ref{fig-evap-usa}--Figure~\ref{fig-evap-china}, which would
raise haulage costs by less than 0.5 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} on
average. The assumption fails for small, isolated patches of one
technology within another, for which there is too little biomass of that
technology nearby to supply a plant; such cells (with \(f < 0.5\)) hold
0--7\% of the adopted biomass, and their biomass would in practice be
used by the surrounding technology.

Haulage costs vary with terrain and road access, which the flat
per-kilometre rate does not capture. For every candidate plant location
and plant size, travel from each field in the collection area to the
plant was routed as the least-time path on the global motorised friction
surface of (\citeproc{ref-weiss2020}{\emph{16}}), at 30 arc-seconds.
Biomass-weighted over the collection area, this gives the travel time
and road-path length per kilometre of straight-line distance, and the
cumulative climb on the loaded and empty legs. The variable trucking
cost was divided into time-based costs (driver wages and benefits, truck
finance, insurance; 64\%), fuel (24\%) and other distance-based costs
(repairs, tyres, tolls; 12\%), following
(\citeproc{ref-atri2024}{\emph{17}}). The time-based share scales with
travel time, fuel with road distance and climb, and the remainder with
road distance. Climb fuel is the energy to lift the loaded and empty
truck over the cumulative ascent of each leg; descents are not credited,
because diesel trucks recover little of the energy on downhill grades.
Each factor is normalised to a biomass-weighted mean of one within each
region, because the regional level of haulage cost is set by the
regional haulage cost factor (Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}). The factors
therefore redistribute haulage cost within a region, from flat,
well-connected areas to rugged or poorly connected ones. Biochar is
returned to the fields as a backhaul in the feedstock trucks, which run
year-round from field-edge storage, so only its loading and handling
cost is charged. Field application (spreading and incorporation) is
costed per hectare at the biochar application rate
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2016optimal}{\emph{18}}), and the diesel used by the
tractor (17.5 L ha\textsuperscript{-1}, the median of published
estimates for spreading and incorporation
(\citeproc{ref-roberts2010}{\emph{19}}--\citeproc{ref-cheng2025}{\emph{22}}))
is counted as an emission. Scope three transportation emissions
associated with feedstock procurement were estimated using a heavy-duty
diesel transport emissions factor applied to the effective distance,
scaled with the fuel factors, and deducted from the net carbon
abatement.

\subsubsection{Fuel quality}\label{fuel-quality}

Agricultural crop residues typically have a higher ash content than the
wood chips more commonly used in bioenergy combustion. High-ash
feedstocks carry significant fouling and slagging risks, requiring
additional capital expenditures (CAPEX) and operating expenditure
(OPEX), for example through deployment of Circulating Fluidized Bed
(CFB) boilers. This fuel quality penalty is modelled, relative to a
wood-chip fired bioenergy plant, as an increase in base CAPEX for the
combustion technologies (BES and BECCS) of 25\% for high-ash feedstocks
(ash above 5\%) or 10\% for medium-ash feedstocks (2--5\%), and a
reduction in electrical conversion efficiency of 10\% or 5\%
respectively (\citeproc{ref-dea2026}{\emph{23}}). O\&M costs are not
penalised separately: they are charged as a fraction of CAPEX, which
already carries the penalty, and straw-fired plants have the same ratio
of O\&M to investment cost as wood-fired plants
(\citeproc{ref-dea2020}{\emph{24}}). The fouling, slagging and corrosion
risks of high-ash, high-alkali residues are reviewed by
(\citeproc{ref-baxter1998}{\emph{25}}),
(\citeproc{ref-garcuxeda2017}{\emph{26}}) and
(\citeproc{ref-luan2025a}{\emph{27}}).

\subsubsection{Regional pricing
structures}\label{regional-pricing-structures}

Feedstock is priced at the field side (farm gate or road side), in 2024
USD per Mg; haulage from the field to the plant is costed separately
(see Biomass transport costs and emissions), so delivered or plant-gate
prices are not used. For Europe, China and India the values are costs of
collection at the field side, excluding the margins of farmers and
intermediaries; for the United States, where no comparable national cost
estimate is available, the value is a farm-gate price that includes the
grower's payment and profit, so it is somewhat higher than a cost-only
estimate would be.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{United States:} US\$50 per dry short ton (2011 USD), or 76.9
  US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in 2024 USD: in the Billion-Ton Update,
  about 80\% of the primary crop residue supply is profitable to collect
  at a farm-gate price of US\$45 per dry ton and 90\% at US\$50
  (\citeproc{ref-perlack2011}{\emph{28}}). The Billion-Ton prices are
  uniform across the country because the underlying POLYSYS runs apply a
  single national price, raised until a large and reliable supply is
  offered. The farm-gate price comprises the harvest cost and a grower
  payment that already compensates for the nutrients removed with the
  residue, so no separate nutrient-replacement charge is added.
\item
  \textbf{Europe:} road-side costs of cereal straw from the S2Biom atlas
  (\citeproc{ref-dees2017s2biom}{\emph{29}}), which maps them at NUTS-3
  level by activity-based costing, aggregated to country level in three
  cost classes: €20 Mg\textsuperscript{-1} dry matter (2012 EUR) for
  eastern Europe, Poland and the Baltic states; €37.5 for most of
  central, northern and southern Europe, including France and Spain; and
  €62.5 for Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg. In 2024
  USD these are 35.1, 65.8 and 109.7 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}.
  Countries without an S2Biom value are assigned by analogy: Kosovo to
  the lowest class, like Serbia and Croatia; Cyprus to the middle class,
  like Greece; and Türkiye and the North African and Middle Eastern
  countries within the European grid to the lowest class, as residue
  costs there are generally below those of most of Europe.
\item
  \textbf{China:} the field-side cost of collecting corn straw estimated
  with the StrawFeed supply-chain model for Nongan county, Jilin
  (\citeproc{ref-wang2022strawfeed}{\emph{30}}): raking, baling and
  loading cost ¥99.6 Mg\textsuperscript{-1} (2019), or 17.7 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in 2024 USD, excluding the ¥72.5
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for transport to the plant. Like the European
  and Indian values, this is a cost and excludes the margins of farmers
  and brokers; power plants in Northeast China pay about ¥300
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} delivered
  (\citeproc{ref-wang2022strawfeed}{\emph{30}}), and ¥200--300
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} at the plant gate in other major producing
  provinces.
\item
  \textbf{India:} paddy straw, which has little value as fodder in the
  rice--wheat belt of the north-west because of its high silica content
  and is otherwise burned in the field, is priced at 34 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} on a dry basis, the cost of baled straw at the
  field side estimated for bioenergy supply chains in Haryana
  (\citeproc{ref-sokhansanj2023}{\emph{31}}). The Monte Carlo analysis
  samples it between about 12 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}, reflecting
  the low value of paddy straw where it is burned
  (\citeproc{ref-erenstein2011}{\emph{32}}), and about 50 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1}, the price of straw traded as fodder
  (\citeproc{ref-duncan2020}{\emph{33}},
  \citeproc{ref-lopes2023}{\emph{34}}). Wheat straw, which is valued as
  fodder, is not used as a feedstock.
\end{itemize}

Year-round operation also requires interim storage of residues harvested
within a few weeks. Published storage costs vary widely, from about 3 to
42 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} (2024 USD): 3--11 for US farm-scale
storage (\citeproc{ref-beach2012}{\emph{14}},
\citeproc{ref-becerra2022}{\emph{35}},
\citeproc{ref-brechbill2008}{\emph{36}}), 18 for on-farm and 31 for
purpose-built storage in the United Kingdom
(\citeproc{ref-shackley2011}{\emph{37}}), 34 for storage and handling
(\citeproc{ref-kung2013}{\emph{38}}), and about 42 for six months'
storage of German straw, where storage is 36.4\% of the total supply
cost (\citeproc{ref-karras2024}{\emph{39}}). We add 15 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for the United States and Europe, sampled from 3
to 42 in the Monte Carlo analysis, and scale it by the regional haulage
cost factor for China and India (11 and 10 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}),
consistent with storage accounting for 20--30\% of the delivered cost of
paddy straw in India (\citeproc{ref-ceew2021exsitu}{\emph{40}}).

\subsection{Technology pathways and
thermodynamics}\label{technology-pathways-and-thermodynamics}

The core thermodynamic engine evaluates three distinct biomass
utilization pathways, processing a biomass input of
\(125\text{ MW}_{\text{th}}\) by default (Table~\ref{tbl-parameters})
into varying ratios of exported electrical energy and sequestered
carbon.

\subsubsection{Bioenergy (BES)}\label{bioenergy-bes}

The BES pathway represents a traditional, dedicated biomass combustion
facility. In this baseline scenario, the feedstock is fully oxidized to
maximize electricity exported to the regional grid. Because there is no
active carbon capture, the physical carbon embedded in the biomass is
returned to the atmosphere.

The technoeconomic parameters for this modern combustion archetype
assume a net electrical efficiency of 30\%
(\citeproc{ref-dea2026}{\emph{23}}). Capital expenditures (CAPEX) are
specified regionally per kW of net electrical capacity for a 50
MW\textsubscript{e} wood-fired reference plant
(Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}), and scale non-linearly with plant capacity
via a standard 0.7 scaling factor. The reference CAPEX (2024 USD) is
4,700 US\$ kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1} for the United
States, from the EIA's 50 MW biomass plant
(\citeproc{ref-eia2026}{\emph{41}}), and 4,300 US\$
kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1} for Europe, from Danish
technology data for a 132 MW\textsubscript{th} straw-fired plant scaled
to 50 MW\textsubscript{e} and adjusted to a wood-fired equivalent by the
Danish straw-to-wood cost ratio (\citeproc{ref-dea2020}{\emph{24}}). For
China and India, where far more biomass plants have been built recently,
it is the average installed cost of bioenergy plants commissioned in
2024, 2,200 and 1,900 US\$ kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1}
(\citeproc{ref-irena2025}{\emph{42}}); India's regulatory benchmark for
rice-straw plants is lower still, about 925 US\$
kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1}
(\citeproc{ref-cerc2024}{\emph{43}}). For costing, plant capacity is
defined as the thermal input multiplied by a fixed reference net
efficiency (\texttt{bes\_capex\_ref\_eff}), rather than by the realised
efficiency. Equipment cost is therefore set by the size of the boiler
and steam cycle, so factors that reduce net output for a given thermal
input (the high-ash fuel penalty, efficiency uncertainty, or the
parasitic load of carbon capture) reduce revenue without spuriously
reducing capital cost. Operations and maintenance (O\&M) costs, fixed
and variable together, are charged as an annual fraction of total CAPEX
(\texttt{plant\_om\_factor}, 4\% per year), which is shared by all
conversion technologies (BES, BECCS, and the pyrolysis unit and energy
block of BEBCS) and the same in every region (see Regional cost
adjustment). The 4\% value is consistent with the EIA reference 50 MW
biomass plant (fixed O\&M of 141.5 US\$ kW\textsuperscript{-1}
yr\textsuperscript{-1} plus variable O\&M of 5.44 US\$
MWh\textsuperscript{-1}, about 3.5\% of CAPEX per year at a 70\%
capacity factor) (\citeproc{ref-eia2023}{\emph{44}}), with IRENA's range
for stoker and fluidized-bed biomass boilers (fixed O\&M of 3.2--4.2\%
of CAPEX per year plus 3.8--4.7 US\$ MWh\textsuperscript{-1})
(\citeproc{ref-irena2012}{\emph{45}}), and with Danish technology data
for 80 MW\textsubscript{th} biomass CHP plants (4.4\% per year for straw
and 4.6\% for wood pellets, fixed and variable combined)
(\citeproc{ref-dea2020}{\emph{24}}). Capital costs are annualized over
the plant lifetime at the regional discount rate using the capital
recovery factor, so that all costs and revenues are expressed as
levelized annual values per megagram of feedstock; because O\&M is a
constant annual cost, discounting it over the plant lifetime and
re-annualizing at the same rate returns the annual value. Total
electrical output is derived directly from the biomass lower heating
value (LHV, \(18.6\text{ GJ Mg}^{-1}\) for dry, ash-free feedstock
(\citeproc{ref-phyllis2}{\emph{46}})) and the plant's capacity factor.
Technology data give an availability of 91\% for biomass plants
(\citeproc{ref-dea2026}{\emph{23}}), but this accounts only for planned
and unplanned outages, not for operation below full output, which
depends on how each technology is dispatched. Capacity factors are
therefore specified per technology (ranges sampled in the Monte Carlo
analysis). Historical capacity factors of biomass power plants, such as
0.54--0.61 for US wood-fired plants over the past decade
(\citeproc{ref-eia2026epm}{\emph{47}}) and 0.50--0.64 for Drax's biomass
units in 2023--2024 (\citeproc{ref-drax2025}{\emph{48}}), reflect
dispatch in grids with low or no carbon prices and are a poor guide to
operation in decarbonising grids. Models that optimise capacity and
hourly dispatch together under carbon constraints find two roles for
biomass power: firm, base-load low-carbon supply
(\citeproc{ref-daggash2019}{\emph{49}},
\citeproc{ref-prado2023}{\emph{50}}), favoured at high
CO\textsubscript{2} charges where other sources of flexibility are ample
(\citeproc{ref-ahlstrom2022}{\emph{8}}), and flexible supply in hours of
high residual load in systems dominated by wind and solar
(\citeproc{ref-johansson2019}{\emph{6}},
\citeproc{ref-lehtveer2021}{\emph{7}}). BES is therefore evaluated in
both modes in the same run, and each grid cell takes the mode with the
higher net value: base load at a capacity factor of 0.85 (range
0.75--0.90) and flexible operation at 0.45 (range 0.30--0.60). Because a
plant that follows prices sells in high-price hours, its realised price
exceeds the time-averaged wholesale price. We estimated this ratio from
hourly day-ahead prices in 24--31 European markets over 2015--2025
(\citeproc{ref-ember2026hourly}{\emph{51}}), for a plant that runs the
highest-priced hours of each day among its available hours (availability
0.91), with perfect foresight and no start-up or minimum-load
constraints. The median ratio across markets in 2024--2025 was 1.05 at a
capacity factor of 0.85 and 1.30 at 0.45 (10th--90th percentile
1.19--1.42); the latter has risen from 1.15--1.21 in 2015--2023 as solar
generation has grown. The daily price spread available to a flexible
generator is bounded by grid storage: prices in discharge hours cannot
persistently exceed the charging price divided by the round-trip
efficiency plus the levelised cost of storage, which at 65 US\$
MWh\textsuperscript{-1} for utility-scale batteries
(\citeproc{ref-ember2025battery}{\emph{52}}) gives about 1.34 times the
average European price (and about 1.9 at the US costs of
(\citeproc{ref-lazard2025}{\emph{53}})). Batteries do not shift energy
between seasons or across multi-day lulls in wind, whereas stored
biomass can, and models of decarbonised systems show a wider spread of
prices with more hours at both very low and high prices
(\citeproc{ref-geis2025}{\emph{54}}). The ratios are therefore 1.05
(range 1.02--1.08) for base load and 1.30 (range 1.15--1.45) for
flexible operation, applied in all regions; they are derived from
European markets and are an assumption elsewhere. Abatement per megagram
of feedstock is the same in both modes, so the choice does not depend on
the carbon price. The allocation between modes also depends on the
generation mix and demand of each grid, which are not modelled. BECCS is
driven towards base-load operation by its higher capital cost, by
removal revenue earned per megagram of CO\textsubscript{2} captured and
by the need to keep the capture plant, dense-phase pipeline and
injection in steady operation (\citeproc{ref-johansson2019}{\emph{6}},
\citeproc{ref-prado2023}{\emph{50}}); it takes 0.85, the design capacity
factor of CCS power plants (\citeproc{ref-petranova2020}{\emph{55}}),
with a range of 0.60--0.90 whose lower end reflects operation of about
3,900 full-load hours where scarce biomass is directed to high-price
hours (\citeproc{ref-lehtveer2021}{\emph{7}}). Electricity is a small
share of BEBCS revenue, so its operation is limited by the availability
of the pyrolysis unit and the continuity of biomass supply rather than
by dispatch; it takes 0.80 (range 0.70--0.85), below the 90\% on-stream
factor of nth-plant pyrolysis designs
(\citeproc{ref-dutta2015}{\emph{56}}) to allow for interruptions of
feedstock supply.

When ash recycling is enabled (the default; \texttt{ash\_recycling},
Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}), the bottom and fly ash from BES and BECCS
combustion is returned to cropland and credited with its liming value,
where soil pH is below the target pH, and its phosphorus fertilizer
value, using the same substitution prices and availability factor as
biochar (see Biochar agronomic valuation). Phosphorus is largely
retained in the ash, whereas nitrogen and most of the potassium are
volatilized during combustion and are not credited. The default ash
liming (calcium carbonate equivalent) and phosphorus contents are set so
that, at the default feedstock ash content, the ash and the biochar
derived from the same feedstock return equal liming and phosphorus value
per megagram of feedstock, so that the pathways differ only in the
nitrogen, potassium and soil physical benefits retained by biochar.

\subsubsection{Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage
(BECCS)}\label{bioenergy-with-carbon-capture-and-storage-beccs}

The BECCS pathway augments the standard combustion baseline with a
post-combustion carbon capture unit. The addition of this unit imposes a
significant parasitic energy penalty, primarily driven by solvent
regeneration and CO\textsubscript{2} compression requirements.
Consequently, the net electrical conversion efficiency drops below the
BES baseline by an efficiency penalty of 8 percentage points (range
5--13), at a capture rate of 90\% (range 85--95\%)
(\citeproc{ref-ieaghg2009}{\emph{57}}). Consistent with costing BECCS as
a mature technology, the central value reflects recent post-combustion
amine capture: reviews place the penalty for pulverised-coal plants at
7.7--11.9 percentage points, and the Boundary Dam 3 unit lost about 8
points with capture (\citeproc{ref-herzog2016}{\emph{58}}); present
technology loses about 10.8 points, falling to 7.2 with improved
solvents, compression and heat integration
(\citeproc{ref-tramosljika2021}{\emph{59}}). The lower bound reflects
projected future penalties of 5.25--6.25 points
(\citeproc{ref-li2024ccs}{\emph{60}}) and the upper bound the 12.8--15.9
points estimated with 2009 amine technology for dedicated biomass plants
(\citeproc{ref-ieaghg2009}{\emph{57}}).

The capture process is parameterized with a 90\% capture efficiency
rate. The total volume of captured CO\textsubscript{2} routed to
geological storage is calculated stoichiometrically based on the carbon
fraction of the incoming biomass feed (default 48\% C
(\citeproc{ref-phyllis2}{\emph{46}})), converted to CO\textsubscript{2}
mass equivalents using the 44/12 molar ratio. The additional
infrastructure (capture unit, CO\textsubscript{2} compression and
flue-gas cleaning) is represented as a premium on total CAPEX relative
to a BES plant of the same thermal input
(\texttt{beccs\_capex\_premium}, Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}). The
default premium is based on the IEAGHG techno-economic evaluation of
dedicated biomass-fired power plants with post-combustion amine capture,
in which capture raised total investment by 38--40\% for 75
MW\textsubscript{e} bubbling and 250 MW\textsubscript{e} circulating
fluidized bed plants (\citeproc{ref-ieaghg2009}{\emph{57}}). Because net
output fell simultaneously, the corresponding increase in specific
capital cost was 114--126\% per net kW\textsubscript{e}, consistent with
the 45--130\% range reported across biomass CCS technologies by
(\citeproc{ref-bhave2017}{\emph{61}}). Expressing the premium on a
thermal-input basis keeps it independent of the efficiency penalty.
Annual O\&M is the same fraction of CAPEX as for the other technologies
(Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}).

\subsection{Carbon transport and geological
storage}\label{carbon-transport-and-geological-storage}

\subsubsection{Storage sites and sink
choice}\label{storage-sites-and-sink-choice}

Geological storage is represented by 45 storage basins across the four
regions (onshore saline formations, onshore oil fields suitable for
CO\textsubscript{2}-enhanced oil recovery (EOR), and offshore saline
formations and depleted fields). Saline storage and EOR are flagged
separately, so that petroleum basins with substantial saline storage
resource (for example the Ordos, Songliao, Tarim and Junggar basins in
China, the Cambay Basin in India and the Permian Basin in the United
States) serve as saline sinks as well as EOR sites. They are listed with
their locations and types in the model repository. Basin locations and
storage types follow the OGCI CO\textsubscript{2} Storage Resource
Catalogue (\citeproc{ref-ogci2024csrc}{\emph{62}}) and, for the United
States, the USGS national assessment of geologic CO\textsubscript{2}
storage resources (\citeproc{ref-usgs2013co2}{\emph{63}}); basins added
for coverage are located at operating or planned storage projects (for
example Porthos, Northern Lights, Greensand, Ravenna CCS, HyNet and
Bayou Bend) listed in the Global CCS Institute's CO2RE facilities
database (\citeproc{ref-gccsi2025co2re}{\emph{64}}). Four
transport-and-storage classes are evaluated for every grid cell:
pipeline to an onshore saline site; pipeline to an EOR site (only when
EOR is allowed, which is not the default); pipeline and ship to an
offshore site; and pipeline, including a subsea section, to an offshore
site. For each cell and plant size, the model selects the class with the
lowest combined transport and storage cost for that plant's
CO\textsubscript{2} flow.

\subsubsection{Pipeline routing}\label{pipeline-routing}

Pipeline routes are computed on a grid ten times finer than the model
grid (0.02° for the United States, 0.01° elsewhere). Two surfaces are
used, one to choose each route and one to cost it, so that the routing
can avoid difficult terrain without that avoidance being charged as
extra pipeline length. Slope from the 250 m Geomorpho90m dataset
(\citeproc{ref-amatulli2020}{\emph{65}}) is converted, per 250 m pixel,
into a construction-cost multiplier \(F_{cost}(\theta)\). This
multiplier is piecewise linear in slope \(\theta\): 1.0 at 0°, 1.05 at
5°, 1.2 at 10°, 1.6 at 17°, 2.3 at 25°, 3.2 at 35° and 4.0 at 45°. The
per-pixel values are then averaged to the routing grid, with additive
premiums above 2,500 m altitude. The routing surface adds a
planning-risk premium for steep ground,
\(F_{route} = F_{cost}\,[1 + r_{max}\,\mathrm{logistic}((\theta - 20^\circ)/3^\circ)]\)
with \(r_{max} = 2\), capped at 25.

Protected areas from the World Database on Protected Areas
(\citeproc{ref-wdpa2026}{\emph{66}}) are tiered. Strictly protected
areas (IUCN categories Ia, Ib and II, and natural World Heritage sites)
are barriers. Other designated terrestrial protected areas can be
crossed at a construction-cost premium of 25\% and a routing premium of
100\%. Marine protected areas are not applied to the land routing.

Least-cost paths on \(F_{route}\) from every cell to the nearest target
were computed with terra (\citeproc{ref-hijmans2025terra}{\emph{12}}).
The resulting route tree was then traced to measure, for each route, its
physical length \(L\), the line integral of \(F_{cost}\) along the
route, and the highest point of the route above the source. Pipeline
cost is charged on the physical length multiplied by the route-average
construction-cost multiplier (the line integral of \(F_{cost}\) divided
by \(L\)). No further tortuosity factor is applied. In a robustness
test, setting \(r_{max}\) between 0 and 2 changed the optimal technology
in less than 0.4\% of grid cells.

\subsubsection{Hub-and-spoke pipeline
modality}\label{hub-and-spoke-pipeline-modality}

Standard length-invariant TEA cost functions for CO\textsubscript{2}
pipelines adequately model regional networks but underestimate the
capital and energy burdens of long-distance transport, which requires
intermediate booster compression to overcome frictional pressure drops.
Pipeline CAPEX is therefore calculated with a piecewise-linear
hub-and-spoke model:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{The Feeder Leg:} Sources build a dedicated feeder pipeline for
  the first 50 km (\(d_{feeder}\)), bearing the full CAPEX scaled to
  their own CO\textsubscript{2} flow (scaling exponent 0.6). Routes of
  50 km or less are dedicated pipelines throughout.
\item
  \textbf{The Trunk Leg (Base Phase):} Up to 700 km, the source connects
  to a regional trunkline operating at an efficient scale (3 Tg
  CO\textsubscript{2} yr\textsuperscript{-1}), paying a share of its
  CAPEX proportional to its mass flow.
\item
  \textbf{The Trunk Leg (Booster Phase):} Beyond 700 km, the marginal
  trunkline cost is doubled (\(P_{booster} = 2.0\)) to approximate the
  capital and operating costs of intermediate booster stations.
\end{enumerate}

The trunkline CAPEX share is:

\[CAPEX_{trunk} = m \cdot
\begin{cases}
C_{base} \cdot (L - 50) & \text{if } L \le 700 \\
C_{base} \cdot (700 - 50) + (C_{base} \cdot P_{booster}) \cdot (L - 700) & \text{if } L > 700
\end{cases}\]

Here \(L\) is the physical route length (km), \(m\) is the route-average
construction-cost multiplier (applied to the feeder leg as well), and
\(C_{base}\) is the unit-length cost of the efficient-scale trunkline,
scaled to the source's share of its mass flow. The reference cost is
US\$50 million per 100 km for a 1 Tg yr\textsuperscript{-1} pipeline,
scaled by the regional CAPEX factor. This lies within the range given by
the diameter-based cost function of
(\citeproc{ref-solomon2024}{\emph{67}}) (2.16 € per m per mm of
diameter, 2024 euros) for the 200--250 mm pipeline needed to carry 1 Tg
yr\textsuperscript{-1} of dense-phase CO\textsubscript{2} at typical
velocities, 0.43--0.54 million € (0.47--0.58 million US\$) per km.
Annual pipeline O\&M is 4\% of CAPEX, and pipeline CAPEX is annualized
over the 25-year plant lifetime at the regional discount rate. Routes
that climb require booster pumping to restore the pressure lost in
lifting dense-phase CO\textsubscript{2}. Pumps are sized for the lift
beyond a 1 MPa design margin, with pump CAPEX following
(\citeproc{ref-mccollum2006}{\emph{68}}), and their electricity is
charged at the regional electricity price.

\subsubsection{Maritime shipping and subsea
pipelines}\label{maritime-shipping-and-subsea-pipelines}

CO\textsubscript{2} destined for offshore sinks is either shipped or
carried by pipeline to the sink:

\[C_{ship} = C_{pipeline}(L_{port}) + C_{liquefaction} + C_{terminal} + c_{voyage} \cdot d_{sea}, \qquad C_{subsea} = C_{pipeline}(L_{landfall} + d_{sea})\]

\(L_{port}\) and \(L_{landfall}\) are the physical lengths of the
onshore routes to the port or landfall. \(d_{sea}\) is the sea distance
from there to the sink, routed on a 0.02--0.04° grid with land
impassable. Liquefaction costs 24.5 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}, from the 21.1--21.3 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
(2021) of the least-cost liquefaction processes for ship transport at 15
bar (\citeproc{ref-chen2021liquefaction}{\emph{69}}), and the fixed cost
of ships, buffer storage and terminals is 15 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
(both scaled by the regional CAPEX factor); the voyage costs 0.010 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} km\textsuperscript{-1}. The fixed and voyage
costs are the intercept and slope of the ship transport cost (excluding
liquefaction) of a 2.5 Tg yr\textsuperscript{-1} point-to-point chain,
8.2 € Mg\textsuperscript{-1} at 180 km rising to 14.5 €
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} at 1,500 km in 2009 euros
(\citeproc{ref-zep2011transport}{\emph{70}}), converted to 2024 US
dollars. For subsea pipelines, the subsea section carries 1.5 times the
onshore CAPEX per km, within the 1.4--1.7 range for offshore relative to
onshore pipelines (\citeproc{ref-vito2026emis}{\emph{71}}).

Every coastal cell is a candidate port or landfall. The land route and
the port are chosen together, by a multi-source least-cost search in
which each coastal cell starts with its sea leg already priced: 0.2
flat-onshore-pipeline-km per sea km for shipping (the ratio of voyage to
trunk-pipeline cost per Mg km), and 1.5 for subsea pipelines. The route
therefore minimizes land and sea cost together rather than land distance
alone. Fixed liquefaction and terminal costs do not affect this choice.

Emissions from CO\textsubscript{2} transport are deducted from the BECCS
abatement. For shipping, these are the emissions from liquefaction,
loading and the voyage, about 2.5\% of the CO\textsubscript{2} carried
over 200 km and about 18\% over 12,000 km
(\citeproc{ref-albaroudi2021}{\emph{72}}). For pipelines, they are the
emissions from the booster pumping electricity, at the displaced grid
intensity.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{CO\textsubscript{2}
injection}{CO2 injection}}\label{co2-injection}

CO\textsubscript{2} injection and storage (injection wells and
operations, and measurement, monitoring and verification) is costed
separately from both the capture plant and CO\textsubscript{2}
transport, as a fixed cost per megagram of CO\textsubscript{2} injected,
with separate values for onshore and offshore storage sites
(\texttt{ccs\_storage\_cost} and \texttt{cost\_offshore\_storage},
Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}). The defaults, 10 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2} onshore (range 2--33) and 20
US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2} offshore (range 5--50),
follow a recent lifecycle assessment of storage costs from exploration
to 50 years of post-closure monitoring. That assessment gives 2--15 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} onshore and 5--31 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
offshore (2024 USD) for reservoirs with open boundary conditions, rising
to 5--33 and 8--50 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for moderate-quality
closed reservoirs, and up to 58 and 147 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for
the least favourable closed reservoirs
(\citeproc{ref-gccsi2025}{\emph{73}}). These ranges are consistent with
earlier estimates for European saline aquifers of €2--12
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} onshore and €6--20 Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
offshore (\citeproc{ref-zep2011}{\emph{74}}). Because boundary
conditions, which depend on the geology, are the main driver of storage
cost (\citeproc{ref-gccsi2025}{\emph{73}}), storage sites are assigned a
site-specific cost where the evidence allows. For onshore saline
formations in the United States, the cost is the first-year break-even
price of the cheapest formation in the sink's state with at least 1 Pg
of prospective storage resource (in the basin, where no formation in the
state qualifies), from a formation-by-formation model that accounts for
pressure build-up and interference
(\citeproc{ref-netl2024scom}{\emph{75}}), escalated from 2023 to 2024
USD. These range from 6.4 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} (Frio Formation,
Gulf Coast) to 22.5 and 24.7 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in the
Denver-Julesburg and Appalachian basins. Elsewhere, saline formations
are classed as open where they are regionally extensive and well
connected (the Utsira and Johansen formations of the northern North Sea,
the Dogger aquifer of the Paris Basin and the Miocene sands of the Gulf
of Mexico shelf), and as closed where they are compartmentalised (the
Buntsandstein of the North German Basin), and take the midpoint of the
corresponding range above (open: 8.5 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} onshore
and 18 offshore; closed: 19 onshore). Depleted oil and gas fields are
not closed aquifers in this sense, because they start below their
original pressure; the six depleted fields among the sinks take 16.2
US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}, the mean of ZEP's offshore cases with and
without re-usable wells (€6 and €10 Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in 2009)
(\citeproc{ref-zep2011}{\emph{74}}) escalated to 2024 USD. Sinks without
sufficient evidence, including all those in China and India, take the
default onshore or offshore values. Site-specific costs are scaled by a
common factor sampled from 0.5 to 1.75 in the Monte Carlo analysis
(\texttt{storage\_cost\_factor}). The applicable cost follows the sink
reached by the route chosen for each grid cell (see Storage sites and
sink choice). Because storage costs differ between sinks, the route
search starts each sink at its storage cost, expressed as an equivalent
length of flat onshore trunk pipeline at 0.05 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} km\textsuperscript{-1} (for example, 160 km for 8
US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}), so that a farther sink with cheaper
storage can be chosen. Injection costs are applied to the mass of
CO\textsubscript{2} captured, in addition to the transport cost and the
capture plant's own CAPEX and O\&M.

\subsection{Bioenergy with biochar storage
(BEBCS)}\label{bioenergy-with-biochar-storage-bebcs}

Unlike the fully oxidative pathways, the BEBCS pathway relies on
thermochemical pyrolysis, producing a solid, recalcitrant carbon matrix
(biochar) alongside volatile syngas and bio-oil. The model employs a
mass and energy balance approach constrained by elemental conservation
of Carbon, Hydrogen, and Oxygen (C, H, O).

The mass yield and elemental composition of the resulting biochar are
determined dynamically as a function of the peak pyrolysis temperature
(\(T_{py}\)) and the lignin fraction of the feedstock. Specific yields
for non-condensable gases hydrogen (\(H_2\)), carbon monoxide (\(CO\)),
methane (\(CH_4\)), and ethylene (\(C_2H_4\)) are calculated based on
temperature-dependent reaction kinetics
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2014}{\emph{76}}). To close the mass balance, the
unaccounted elemental fractions (\(U_c, U_h, U_o\)) are
stoichiometrically partitioned into bio-oil, water (\(H_2O\)), and
carbon dioxide (CO\textsubscript{2}). Bio-oil C and H mass fractions are
set at 1.25 and 1.26 times those of the feedstock
(e.g.~\(62.5\%\text{ C}\) and \(7.56\%\text{ H}\) for a feedstock with
50\% C and 6\% H), allowing the final product distribution to be solved
analytically (\citeproc{ref-woolf2014}{\emph{76}}). The default peak
pyrolysis temperature of 500 °C is a modelling assumption representing
the medium-temperature class (450--600 °C) of
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2021greenhouse}{\emph{77}}) and the IPCC inventory
guidance (\citeproc{ref-ipcc2019biochar}{\emph{78}}); it is sampled
±10\% in the Monte Carlo analysis. All yields and energy flows are
expressed per megagram of dry, ash-free feedstock, with feedstock ash
reporting to the biochar.

The net usable energy flux for the BEBCS pathway is calculated from an
explicit energy balance on a consistent higher heating value (HHV)
basis. The process heat demand is the HHV of the products (biochar,
bio-oil and permanent gases) plus explicit process heat losses, minus
the HHV of the feedstock. The HHVs of biochar and bio-oil are calculated
via the Dulong formula from their C, H and O fractions, and that of the
gas from the heats of combustion of its components. Heat losses comprise
conduction through the insulated reactor wall (sized from the plant feed
rate and a temperature-dependent solids residence time), the sensible
heat of biochar discharged at the pyrolysis temperature, and the latent
and sensible heat of the bio-oil vapour, permanent gases and water
vapour (feedstock moisture plus reaction water) leaving the
heat-recovery system. Because the water vapour term includes the latent
heat of all water leaving the process, expressing both feedstock and
products on an HHV basis avoids double-counting that latent heat.
Process heat is supplied by combusting the syngas and bio-oil vapours in
a process heater (80\% efficiency, HHV basis), and the plant's parasitic
electricity demand (0.07 GJ\textsubscript{e} per Mg feed) is met from
the same fuel (\citeproc{ref-woolf2014}{\emph{76}}). If the syngas and
bio-oil are insufficient to meet these loads, a portion of the biochar
is oxidized to close the energy balance, proportionally reducing the
final carbon sequestration yield. The remaining fuel, converted to a
lower heating value basis, is used to generate electricity in a
dedicated power block (35\% net electrical efficiency, LHV basis
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2014}{\emph{76}})), whose capital cost is sized on
its actual fuel input using the same reference-plant scaling as the
combustion pathways. A power block is not always worthwhile: where
electricity prices are low or the displaced grid intensity is small, its
capital cost can exceed its revenue and grid credit. BEBCS is therefore
evaluated in each grid cell both with the power block and without any
energy co-product, in which case the surplus vapours and gases are
flared (process heat and the parasitic load are still met from the
pyrolysis fuel), and takes the configuration with the higher net value
at the prevailing carbon price.

The pyrolysis unit CAPEX is 335 US\$ per Mg yr\textsuperscript{-1} of
feed capacity in the United States (2024 USD), for the feed of a 50
MW\textsubscript{e} reference plant, from NREL's nth-plant estimate for
a 2,000 Mg day\textsuperscript{-1} (dry basis) slow pyrolysis plant
(\citeproc{ref-yadav2026}{\emph{79}}) scaled to the reference size with
an exponent of 0.7. The power block, which burns the pyrolysis vapours
and gases and raises steam for a condensing turbine, costs 3,250 US\$
kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1} in the United States. This lies
between the cost of a steam cycle alone in NREL's pyrolysis designs,
about 2,650 US\$ kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1}
(\citeproc{ref-dutta2015}{\emph{56}}), and that of a complete 50 MW
biomass power plant, which also includes solid-fuel handling (4,700 US\$
kW\textsubscript{e}\textsuperscript{-1};
(\citeproc{ref-eia2026}{\emph{41}})). Both costs are scaled to other
regions by the capital cost location factor (see Regional cost
adjustment).

\subsubsection{Biochar permanence}\label{biochar-permanence}

The Bioenergy with Biochar Storage (BEBCS) pathway diverts a fraction of
the original biomass carbon into a recalcitrant solid matrix. While
physical disposal methods such as the landfilling of biochar represent a
highly effective method to provide highly stable carbon storage, this
framework strictly evaluates soil application to quantify the cascading
agronomic co-benefits and regional substitution values. Consequently,
the carbon retention within the soil matrix is finite and must be
modeled dynamically based on localized biogeochemical conditions.

\emph{Biochar carbon permanence (\(F_{perm}\)).} To accurately quantify
the net carbon dioxide removal (CDR) of the BEBCS pathway, the model
calculates the permanent carbon fraction (\(F_{perm}\)) that remains
stabilized in the soil after a 100-year time horizon. This fraction is
derived using the multi-pool decay methodology established by
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2021greenhouse}{\emph{77}}).

The baseline degradation rates of the biochar's labile and recalcitrant
carbon pools are fitted to either the peak pyrolysis temperature
(\(T_{py}\)) or the molar Hydrogen-to-Organic-Carbon (H:C) ratio of the
final char. The model uses the H:C ratio, derived from the
temperature-dependent biochar composition, so that biochar yield and
permanence respond jointly to \(T_{py}\). Because microbial and chemical
degradation kinetics are highly temperature-dependent, these reference
decay rates are spatially adjusted to match the localized mean annual
soil temperature using a \(Q_{10}\) temperature sensitivity function. To
optimize spatial computational efficiency across the high-resolution
rasters, the thermodynamic module utilizes bilinear interpolation over a
pre-calculated matrix of \(F_{perm}\) values bounded by soil
temperatures (\(-55^\circ\text{C}\) to \(40^\circ\text{C}\)) and H:C
ratios (\(0.0\) to \(0.7\)).

Total carbon sequestered per megagram of feedstock is therefore modeled
as:

\[C_{seq} = Yield_{bc} \cdot C_{content} \cdot F_{perm} \cdot \left(\frac{44}{12}\right)\]

where \(Yield_{bc}\) is the mass fraction of biochar produced,
\(C_{content}\) is the carbon mass fraction of the biochar, and the
\(44/12\) ratio converts the elemental carbon to CO\textsubscript{2}
mass equivalents.

\emph{Soil N\textsubscript{2}O emission reduction.} Biochar additions to
soil can suppress nitrous oxide (N\textsubscript{2}O) emissions from
nitrogen fertilizer. From field trials with biochar additions exceeding
10 Mg C ha\textsuperscript{-1},
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2021greenhouse}{\emph{77}}) estimated a 23\%
reduction in N\textsubscript{2}O emissions (95\% confidence interval
5--41\%). The model credits this fractional reduction (\(R_{N_2O}\))
against direct fertilizer-induced N\textsubscript{2}O emissions on land
receiving biochar at that application rate (\(A_{bc}\)), where direct
emissions are the regional mineral N application rate (\(N_{app}\))
multiplied by the IPCC Tier 1 emission factor (\(EF_1\))
(\citeproc{ref-ipcc2019}{\emph{80}}). Because significant effects have
only been demonstrated in the first year after application
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2021greenhouse}{\emph{77}}), the reduction is
assumed to persist for a short period (\(t_{N_2O}\)), with a long upper
tail in the Monte Carlo analysis to reflect the uncertainty. The credit
per megagram of feedstock is:

\[A_{N_2O} = \frac{N_{app} \cdot EF_1 \cdot R_{N_2O} \cdot t_{N_2O} \cdot C_{bc}}{A_{bc}} \cdot \frac{44}{28} \cdot GWP_{N_2O} \cdot 10^{-3}\]

where \(C_{bc}\) is the biochar carbon yield (Mg C per Mg feedstock),
\(GWP_{N_2O}\) is the 100-year global warming potential of
N\textsubscript{2}O (\citeproc{ref-forster2021}{\emph{81}}), and
parameter values are given in Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}. Because the
effect is short-lived and spread over the large area to which biochar is
applied, this credit is small relative to the carbon stored in the
biochar.

\subsubsection{Biochar agronomic
valuation}\label{biochar-agronomic-valuation}

Previous spatial models frequently value biochar using a static,
exogenous global market price. To better reflect real-world agricultural
economics, C-SCAPE endogenously determines the economic credit of
biochar through a mechanistic substitution valuation. This assumes that
the shadow price of biochar is equal to the cost of the conventional
agricultural inputs it offsets:

\[Value_{ag} = Value_{lime} + Value_{nutrients} + Value_{physical}\]

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Liming Equivalence (}\(Value_{lime}\)): The model spatially
  evaluates the native soil pH against a neutral agricultural target (pH
  6.5). If the soil is acidic, the biochar's Calcium Carbonate
  Equivalent (CCE, default 15\%) is credited against the cost of the
  agricultural lime it displaces. Because substitution also avoids
  hauling and spreading the lime, this cost includes transport and
  application. It is the median of published values, each converted to a
  calcium carbonate equivalent of 100\% and escalated to 2024 US
  dollars: 59 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}, ranging from 31 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for lime delivered to Brazilian pastures,
  excluding spreading (\citeproc{ref-latawiec2019}{\emph{82}}), through
  52 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in Oklahoma
  (\citeproc{ref-mills2020lime}{\emph{83}}) and 59--110 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} near to and far from a quarry in Oklahoma
  (\citeproc{ref-cho2020lime}{\emph{84}}), to 85 US\$
  Mg\textsuperscript{-1} spread in North Carolina
  (\citeproc{ref-woods2026}{\emph{85}}). The range is sampled in the
  Monte Carlo analysis. If the regional soil is already alkaline, the
  liming value is strictly zero.
\item
  \textbf{Nutrient Substitution (}\(Value_{nutrients}\)): The available
  nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) fractions within the
  biochar are credited against prevailing regional fertilizer costs.
  Nutrients are valued at the world market prices of standard commercial
  fertilizers: urea for N (1.06 US\$ kg\textsuperscript{-1} N), triple
  superphosphate for P (1.27 US\$ kg\textsuperscript{-1}
  P\textsubscript{2}O\textsubscript{5}) and potassium chloride for K
  (0.86 US\$ kg\textsuperscript{-1} K\textsubscript{2}O). These are
  averages of the World Bank's annual prices for 2021--2025, each
  escalated to 2024 USD
  (\citeproc{ref-worldbank2026pinksheet}{\emph{86}}). The same prices
  are used in every region: retail subsidies (as in India) and export
  controls (as in China) move farm-gate prices away from world prices,
  but they transfer value rather than change the economic value of the
  fertilizer displaced. Elemental P and K contents are converted to
  their oxide equivalents (×2.29 for
  P\textsubscript{2}O\textsubscript{5} and ×1.20 for
  K\textsubscript{2}O) before pricing, and each nutrient is credited at
  its first-year plant availability (N 10\%, P 50\%, K 80\%;
  Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}).
\item
  \textbf{Physical/CEC Improvements (}\(Value_{physical}\)): Biochar
  raises the cation exchange capacity (CEC) of soils, improving their
  retention and supply of nutrients and water; the effect is largest in
  sandy or degraded soils. Unlike liming, this benefit is valued as a
  yield increment rather than as a substitute input. Agricultural lime
  is an equivalent substitute for biochar (or ash) as a pH amendment, so
  the liming benefit is credited at the cost of the lime it displaces.
  Fertilizer, however, is not a substitute for CEC but a complementary
  input: a soil with low CEC cannot retain and supply nutrients
  effectively and responds poorly to fertilizer, so raising its CEC
  lifts the whole fertilizer response curve, including the economically
  optimal application rate. The annual yield benefit is 21.1 US\$ per Mg
  of biochar carbon in soils with a CEC of 5 cmol\textsubscript{c}
  kg\textsuperscript{-1} or less. It is derived from the mean crop-yield
  benefit of 30 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} C yr\textsuperscript{-1} in
  (\citeproc{ref-woolf2016optimal}{\emph{18}}) (average 2008--2013
  prices), of which about half is attributable to CEC and half to pH
  (the latter credited separately as the liming value), escalated to
  2024 USD. The benefit falls linearly with increasing CEC to zero at 30
  cmol\textsubscript{c} kg\textsuperscript{-1}; this linear scaling is a
  heuristic.
\end{enumerate}

Liming and nutrient values are one-off credits per megagram of biochar
applied. The CEC of biochar increases rather than declines as it ages in
soil, through oxidation and functionalization of its surfaces, so the
physical benefit is treated as persisting for as long as the biochar
carbon remains. Its present value is the annual benefit divided by the
sum of the regional discount rate and the decay rate of biochar carbon,
the latter being the mean rate implied by the 100-year permanence factor
(\(k = -\ln F_{perm}/100\); see Biochar permanence).

\subsection{Baseline energy system and
offsets}\label{baseline-energy-system-and-offsets}

To robustly account for the climate mitigation benefit of bioenergy
generation, the framework evaluates greenhouse gas offsets using a
consequential Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) boundary. Total carbon
abatement is calculated distinctively for each technology pathway,
balancing active sequestration, grid displacement credits, and Scope 3
supply chain emissions.

\subsubsection{Net carbon abatement
balances}\label{net-carbon-abatement-balances}

The net carbon abatement for each grid cell is defined as the sum of
physical carbon sequestered and emissions displaced, minus the
transportation emissions penalty:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{BES:} Abatement relies entirely on the displacement of grid
  emissions, minus the Scope 3 emissions associated with diesel trucking
  of the biomass feedstock.
\item
  \textbf{BECCS:} Abatement includes the physical CO\textsubscript{2}
  captured and injected into the geological sink (assuming a 90\%
  capture rate) plus the grid displacement credit. However, because the
  parasitic energy load of the capture process reduces the net
  electrical efficiency, the total energy exported and thus the
  resulting grid displacement credit is proportionally lower for BECCS
  than for pure BES.
\item
  \textbf{BEBCS:} Abatement combines the grid displacement credit with
  the permanent CO\textsubscript{2} equivalent of the biochar carbon
  retained in the soil (\(F_{perm}\)) and the avoided soil
  N\textsubscript{2}O emissions (\(A_{N_2O}\)), minus the diesel
  emissions of biochar field application.
\end{itemize}

For BECCS, the emissions of CO\textsubscript{2} transport are also
deducted (see Maritime shipping and subsea pipelines). The residue
removal counterfactual below applies to all pathways.

For all pathways, Scope 3 feedstock transport emissions are modeled
dynamically by applying a heavy-duty diesel transport emissions factor
(\(0.0001\text{ Mg CO}_2\text{e}\) per \(\text{Mg}\cdot\text{km}\), the
well-to-wheel default for a 34--40 Mg articulated diesel truck at
average load, including empty running
(\citeproc{ref-glec2024}{\emph{87}})) to the effective biomass
collection distance.

\subsubsection{Residue removal
counterfactual}\label{residue-removal-counterfactual}

Removing crop residues for energy has two further effects on greenhouse
gas balances that are common to all pathways, relative to a
counterfactual in which residues remain in the field. First, retained
residues add to soil organic carbon (SOC); residue retention increases
SOC stocks by around 10\% relative to removal
(\citeproc{ref-bolinder2020}{\emph{88}}). Retained residues also add
nitrogen to the soil and increase soil N\textsubscript{2}O emissions.
Process-based simulations across more than 400 million hectares of
cropland found that, through to 2100, the SOC gain and the
N\textsubscript{2}O increase from retaining more residue approximately
cancel almost everywhere, the Brazilian Cerrado being the main exception
(\citeproc{ref-mcclelland2025}{\emph{9}}). The net soil GHG penalty of
removal (\(P_{soil}\), Mg CO\textsubscript{2}e per Mg feed) is therefore
set to zero by default, and retained as a parameter for sensitivity
analysis (Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}). A penalty that counted the
forgone SOC alone, without the offsetting N\textsubscript{2}O, would be
about 0.19 Mg CO\textsubscript{2}e per Mg feed. Second, in regions where
residues are commonly burned in the field, removal avoids the
non-CO\textsubscript{2} emissions from open burning (CO\textsubscript{2}
from burning is biogenic and is not counted). These are estimated with
the IPCC Tier 1 emission factors for methane and nitrous oxide from
burning agricultural residues and the associated combustion factor
(\citeproc{ref-ipcc2006}{\emph{89}}), applied to the share of the
available residue in each grid cell that would otherwise be burned
(\(f_{burn}\)). The burnt mass is estimated from the burnt share of
cereal residue production in each 0.5° cell (2017--2021 mean) of a
global cereal residue dataset that combines satellite fire observations
with residue production (\citeproc{ref-smerald2023}{\emph{90}}),
multiplied by the cereal field residue production underlying the residue
supply (\citeproc{ref-karan2023}{\emph{10}}); \(f_{burn}\) is this mass
divided by the available residue, both averaged to the model grid, and
capped at 1. Using the burnt share rather than the burnt mass keeps the
different residue totals of the two datasets out of the ratio. Burning
of non-cereal residues, such as sugarcane trash, is not counted, and
satellite fire products under-detect small field fires, so the estimate
is conservative; a multiplier sampled from 0.5 to 1.5 represents this
uncertainty in the Monte Carlo analysis. The biomass-weighted mean of
\(f_{burn}\) is 2\% in the United States, 6\% in Europe, 3\% in China
and 14\% in India, and burning is concentrated in Punjab and Haryana in
India, Ukraine and the Balkans in Europe, and north-eastern China:

\[A_{residue} = f_{burn} \cdot CF \cdot \left(EF_{CH_4} \cdot GWP_{CH_4} + EF_{N_2O} \cdot GWP_{N_2O}\right) \cdot 10^{-3} - P_{soil}\]

where \(CF\) is the combustion factor, \(EF\) the emission factors (g
kg\textsuperscript{-1} dry matter), and \(GWP\) the 100-year global
warming potentials (\citeproc{ref-forster2021}{\emph{81}}); parameter
values are given in Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}. Because the same term
applies to every pathway, it does not change which technology is
preferred in a grid cell, but it changes the absolute net abatement and
therefore shifts the marginal abatement cost curves.

\subsubsection{Grid displacement}\label{grid-displacement}

The carbon intensity of the electricity displaced by bioenergy output
depends on how far the energy system has decarbonized, which in turn
depends on the carbon price. Because the carbon price \(P\) is an
exogenous input to the analysis, the displaced marginal emission factor
(MEF) is modeled as a continuous function of \(P\), so that the model
does not combine a high carbon price with a fossil-dominated grid
margin. Each grid cell's empirical build margin at today's explicit
carbon price is used as an anchor and scaled by a regional
price-response curve calibrated to integrated assessment scenarios.

\textbf{Empirical anchor.} New bioenergy capacity is assumed to displace
the technology mix currently expanding on each grid. Using monthly
national and US state electricity generation data
(\citeproc{ref-ember2025}{\emph{91}}), the sources with positive net
generation growth (\(\Delta G_i > 0\)) between 2019 and 2024 are
identified, and the build-margin intensity is their growth-weighted
mean:

\[MEF_{emp} = \frac{\sum_i \Delta G_{i} \cdot CI_{i}}{\sum_i \Delta G_{i}}\]

where \(CI_i\) is the IPCC median life-cycle greenhouse gas intensity of
source \(i\) (\citeproc{ref-schlomer2014}{\emph{92}}): coal (820 g
CO\textsubscript{2}e kWh\textsuperscript{-1}), natural gas (490), solar
(48), geothermal (38), hydro (24), nuclear (12) and wind (11.5).
Bioenergy generation is assigned zero intensity, because bioenergy
emissions are accounted for explicitly in the model; growth in bioenergy
generation still counts in the denominator. Where no growth data are
available, the average grid intensity is used. The resulting anchors are
378 g CO\textsubscript{2}e kWh\textsuperscript{-1} for China and 574 for
India, with medians over biomass-producing grid cells of 341 for the
United States (state level) and 38 for Europe (country level).

\textbf{Price-response curve.} The decline of the displaced intensity
with carbon price follows a Hill (log-logistic) curve,

\[MEF(P) = MEF_{min} + \left(MEF_{emp} - MEF_{min}\right) \frac{H(P)}{H(P_{now})}, \qquad H(P) = \frac{1}{1 + (P/P_{50})^{k}}\]

where \(P_{now}\) is the region's current explicit carbon price on
power-sector emissions (Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}: the EU Emissions
Trading System and China's national emissions trading scheme; no
national price in the United States or India), so that the curve passes
through each cell's empirical anchor at today's price. The scenario
carbon price is thus interpreted as an explicit price: for a given
explicit price, the curve gives the intensity of the electricity that
new bioenergy capacity would displace, with today's non-price policies
embedded in the empirical anchor. Prices below today's level imply a
more carbon-intensive margin than observed, capped at the intensity of
coal. \(MEF_{min}\) = 20 g CO\textsubscript{2}e kWh\textsuperscript{-1}
is the residual life-cycle intensity of a decarbonized grid
(\citeproc{ref-pehl2017}{\emph{93}}). The midpoint price \(P_{50}\) and
steepness \(k\) were fitted for each region to the NGFS Phase 5
scenarios (seven scenarios; GCAM 6.0 and MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM 2.0)
(\citeproc{ref-ngfs2026}{\emph{94}}), by robust least squares with equal
model weights. GCAM reports the United States, India and the European
Union as native regions. For MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM, whose native regions are
larger aggregates, the NGFS country-level downscaled results for the
United States, India and the EU-27 were used, with coal and gas
generation divided into unabated and CCS shares according to the parent
region. For each model, the fleet-average intensity of electricity
generation at each five-year step was normalized by that model's own
low-price value, so that only the shape of the decline, and not its
level, is transferred to the empirical anchors. The fleet average was
used rather than the models' build margin because, in these scenarios,
new capacity is already predominantly renewable at zero carbon price,
leaving the build margin with little price response; the fleet average
tracks the decarbonization of the system that a baseload plant displaces
over its lifetime. A two-stage variant (coal-to-gas switching, then
decarbonization) was selected by the Akaike information criterion for
Europe only; single-stage curves were imposed for China and India, where
coal-to-gas switching is limited by gas availability. The fitted
midpoints are \(P_{50}\) = 103 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}
CO\textsubscript{2}e for the United States, 53 for China and 44 for
India, and 45 and 138 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} for the two stages in
Europe (2024 USD). REMIND-MAgPIE was excluded from the fit because its
grid decarbonizes over time even at low carbon prices, so that its price
response could not be separated from its time trend. Parameter
uncertainty is represented by 1,000 bootstrap draws over models and
scenarios, which are sampled in the Monte Carlo analysis. Because the
same carbon price values removals and emissions, the displaced intensity
declines as the removal price rises.

\subsection{Regional cost adjustment}\label{regional-cost-adjustment}

The combustion plant CAPEX of BES and BECCS is specified for each region
directly (see Bioenergy (BES)). Other capital costs (pyrolysis plants,
the BEBCS power block, pipelines, liquefaction and port terminals) are
specified for a base location and scaled by a regional capital cost
location factor, so that the relative cost of technologies is consistent
within each region. O\&M is charged at the same fraction of CAPEX in
every region, so its absolute level follows regional CAPEX. The fraction
does not fall where labour is cheaper: India's regulated O\&M allowance
for biomass plants is 7.8\% of the regulated CAPEX per year
(\citeproc{ref-cerc2024}{\emph{43}}), compared with about 4\% in the
United States and Europe (\citeproc{ref-dea2020}{\emph{24}},
\citeproc{ref-eia2026}{\emph{41}}). Biomass haulage costs are scaled by
a regional road-freight cost factor (Table~\ref{tbl-parameters}).

\subsection{Spatial input data}\label{spatial-input-data}

Crop residue availability is the sustainably harvestable residue of
(\citeproc{ref-karan2023}{\emph{10}}) (net of residues retained for soil
protection), minus the regional fraction used as livestock fodder and
bedding (\citeproc{ref-herrero2013}{\emph{11}}). Feedstock composition
is representative of cereal straws, corn stover and rice straw in the
Phyllis2 database (\citeproc{ref-phyllis2}{\emph{46}}): 48\% carbon and
a lower heating value of 18.6 GJ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} on a dry,
ash-free basis, with ash 5\% of dry mass in the United States and
Europe, where wheat straw and corn stover predominate, and 15\% in China
and India, where rice straw makes up a large share. Soil pH and cation
exchange capacity (0--5 cm) are from SoilGrids 2.0
(\citeproc{ref-poggio2021}{\emph{95}}) and mean annual soil temperature
(0--5 cm) from the global soil bioclimatic maps of
(\citeproc{ref-lembrechts2022}{\emph{96}}). Electricity is valued at the
wholesale price a generator receives, rather than at retail tariffs,
whose regional differences largely reflect taxes, network charges and
cross-subsidies. Prices are averages over 2024--2025, excluding the
anomalous prices of the 2022 European gas crisis and its aftermath in
2023. For the United States, each state takes the price at the wholesale
trading hub of the market that serves most of it (11 hubs)
(\citeproc{ref-eia2026steo}{\emph{97}}). For Europe, prices are national
day-ahead market prices (\citeproc{ref-ember2026price}{\emph{98}}), with
those of Türkiye (\citeproc{ref-epias2026}{\emph{99}}) and Ukraine
(\citeproc{ref-marketoperator2026}{\emph{100}}) from their market
operators; small markets without published prices take a neighbouring
market's price. Belarus, which has no wholesale market, takes its
industrial tariff (\citeproc{ref-climatescope2021}{\emph{101}})
multiplied by the ratio of wholesale to industrial prices in the United
States (median across states in 2024, 0.42)
(\citeproc{ref-eia2026steo}{\emph{97}},
\citeproc{ref-eia2025epa}{\emph{102}}). For China, the price is the
provincial benchmark on-grid tariff for coal-fired power
(\citeproc{ref-ndrc2019}{\emph{103}}), which is also the reference price
for renewable generators, excluding value-added tax. For India, it is
the average price on the day-ahead market of the Indian Energy Exchange
in 2024-25 (\citeproc{ref-cerc2025stm}{\emph{104}}), a single national
value because the market rarely separates into regional prices. Each
year's price is converted at that year's average exchange rate
(\citeproc{ref-worldbank2026fx}{\emph{105}}) and escalated to 2024 US
dollars. The biomass-weighted mean prices are 40 US\$
MWh\textsuperscript{-1} for the United States, 96 for Europe, 47 for
China and 49 for India. Grid carbon intensities are described under Grid
Displacement.

\subsection{Economic evaluation and technology
selection}\label{economic-evaluation-and-technology-selection}

For each grid cell and technology, the net value per megagram of
feedstock is the sum of revenues (electricity sales, the carbon price
multiplied by the net abatement, and the agronomic value of biochar or
ash) minus costs. The costs are annualized CAPEX, O\&M, feedstock and
haulage, and, for BECCS, CO\textsubscript{2} transport and storage.
CAPEX is annualized with the capital recovery factor at the regional
discount rate over the technology lifetime (25 years for BES and BECCS
(\citeproc{ref-dea2026}{\emph{23}}); 20 years for the pyrolysis unit and
25 years for its power block
(\citeproc{ref-woolf2016optimal}{\emph{18}})). The regional discount
rates are real, after-tax costs of capital for bioenergy projects: 5.6\%
for the United States, 6.8\% for Europe, 6.2\% for China and 7.1\% for
India. They are the nominal costs of capital in US dollars projected for
2030 by (\citeproc{ref-hatton2025}{\emph{106}}), converted to real terms
with an expected inflation rate of 2.5\%. We use after-tax rates, as is
usual in techno-economic assessment. Grossing them up to pre-tax rates
at statutory corporate tax rates (\citeproc{ref-enache2024}{\emph{107}})
would add 3--4 percentage points, but would overstate the effective tax
burden, which depreciation allowances reduce. Real rates are required
because all cash flows, including the carbon price, are expressed in
constant 2024 US dollars. All costs are in 2024 US dollars: values from
other years are escalated with the US consumer price index (CPI-U), and
values in other currencies are converted at the average exchange rate of
the source year before escalation. The plant has a thermal input of 125
MW\textsubscript{th} by default, and the preferred technology in each
cell is the one with the highest net value. Because the displaced grid
intensity falls as the carbon price rises (see Grid displacement), net
abatement, and hence net value, is not linear in the carbon price. We
therefore evaluate the model on a grid of carbon prices (0--250 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e in steps of 5 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1}, and 275--500 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1} in
steps of 25 US\$ Mg\textsuperscript{-1}) and interpolate abatement
linearly between grid points; net value is the carbon-price-independent
part plus the carbon price multiplied by the interpolated abatement (for
BEBCS, both parts are those of the configuration chosen at each price).
The break-even carbon price of a technology is the lowest carbon price
at which its net value becomes non-negative. Marginal abatement cost
curves assign each grid cell, at each carbon price, to the technology
with the highest net value if that value is non-negative, and sum the
biomass, area and abatement of the assigned cells.

\subsection{Uncertainty and sensitivity
analyses}\label{uncertainty-and-sensitivity-analyses}

Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation, with
5,000 draws per region. Each parameter with an uncertainty range was
sampled from a PERT or uniform distribution, with the central values,
distributions and bounds listed in Table~\ref{tbl-si-mc} (see
Statistical methods), and correlated parameters were sampled jointly
through a Gaussian copula. The uncertainty of the grid displacement
curve was represented by sampling one of its 1,000 bootstrap draws in
each iteration. The drivers of each technology's net value were
identified by fitting gradient-boosted regression trees {[}XGBoost;
(\citeproc{ref-chen2016xgboost}{\emph{108}}){]} to the Monte Carlo
results and computing SHAP values
(\citeproc{ref-lundberg2017}{\emph{109}}). The same method was applied
across grid cells, to the choice of preferred technology, to identify
its spatial drivers. Structural assumptions that the Monte Carlo
analysis holds fixed were tested with the same carbon-price sweep as the
marginal abatement cost curves, at the regional discount rates: plant
sizes of 50 and 250 MW\textsubscript{th} either side of the default of
125 MW\textsubscript{th}, dedicated pipelines instead of shared
trunklines (each plant builds its own pipeline over the full distance to
the sink), and CO\textsubscript{2}-EOR allowed as a sink
(Figure~\ref{fig-si-variants}). Plant size is treated as a design
assumption rather than optimised. When the model chooses the size that
maximises net value in each cell, it tends towards the largest size
considered, because economies of scale outweigh the longer haulage,
implying gigawatt-scale plants throughout agricultural landscapes. In
practice, plant size is limited by constraints outside the model, such
as the road traffic and nuisance of continuous truck deliveries and the
capacity of local roads and grid connections, so the default of 125
MW\textsubscript{th} is large compared with typical biomass plants but
not exceptional, and the size variants show the sensitivity of the
results to this assumption.

\subsection{Statistical methods}\label{statistical-methods}

Table~\ref{tbl-si-mc} lists every sampled parameter with its central
value, distribution and bounds. The table is generated directly from the
model's canonical parameter file
(\texttt{BiocharAG/inst/extdata/parameters.csv}), so it always matches
the values used in the simulations. Ranges marked ``× central'' are
multipliers of the central value (which may differ by region); other
ranges are in the parameter's units. Where a bound differs by region it
is shown in parentheses. For spatially explicit inputs (for example the
electricity price), the relative range is applied as a multiplier on the
spatial layer.

To identify which spatial inputs determine the optimal technology in
each grid cell, we fitted a surrogate classifier to the spatial
sensitivity results (scenario CP100\_MW250: carbon price 100 US\$
Mg\textsuperscript{-1} CO\textsubscript{2}e, plant size 250
MW\textsubscript{th}) and attributed its predictions to the inputs with
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values
(\citeproc{ref-chen2016xgboost}{\emph{108}}). The classifier is a
gradient-boosted tree ensemble (XGBoost; multi-class soft-probability
objective, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.05, 120 rounds) that
predicts the technology with the highest net value (BES, BECCS or BEBCS)
from ten cell-level inputs: biomass density, soil temperature,
electricity price, field-side feedstock cost, the length of the
CO\textsubscript{2} route chosen by BECCS, whether that route ends at an
offshore sink, the storage cost at that sink, soil pH, soil cation
exchange capacity (CEC) and the displaced grid carbon intensity. SHAP
values are in log-odds of each class.

Two kinds of model are fitted. A global model, fitted to all four
regions together, shows which inputs distinguish the regions as well as
cells within them. Because some inputs are constant within a region (for
example, feedstock cost outside Europe and the electricity price in
India), in the global model they partly act as labels for the region
rather than as drivers of within-region variation. A separate model is
therefore fitted for each region; inputs that are constant within the
region are dropped. The regional models are used for the maps of the
dominant driver (Figure~\ref{fig-shap-dominant},
Figure~\ref{fig-shap-magnitude}) and for the regional beeswarm and
dependence plots (Figure~\ref{fig-si-bee-global} to
Figure~\ref{fig-si-bee-india} and the dependence plots that follow
them); Table~\ref{tbl-si-shap-importance} gives the share of each input
in the mean absolute SHAP value of each model. Beeswarm and dependence
plots show a random sample of 20,000 cells per model; SHAP values were
computed for all cells. In the dependence plots, points are coloured by
the input with the strongest estimated interaction with the plotted
input.
