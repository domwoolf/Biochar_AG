1 Introduction

1.1 Core Objective

The BiocharAG package provides a spatially explicit Technoeconomic Analysis (TEA) framework designed to evaluate and compare the financial viability of biomass-driven carbon dioxide removal (CDR) and energy generation strategies. The model identifies the optimal use of regional biomass resources by calculating the net present value (NPV) of competing technology pathways at a highly granular, grid-cell level.

1.2 Technology Pathways Evaluated

The model evaluates three distinct biomass utilization pathways. Each pathway processes the same initial biomass feedstock but yields different energy outputs, carbon sequestration profiles, and capital requirements:

  • Bioenergy (BES): The baseline scenario. Biomass is fully combusted to maximize energy export to the grid. Carbon is released back into the atmosphere (carbon neutral, but no active sequestration).
  • Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS): Biomass is combusted for energy, but the exhaust gas is routed through a carbon capture unit. The captured \(CO_2\) is compressed, transported, and injected into a geological sink. This pathway imposes a severe “parasitic load” on energy generation but yields high tradable carbon credits.
  • Bioenergy with Biochar Storage (BEBCS): Biomass undergoes pyrolysis. A portion of the carbon is stabilized as biochar (a solid, recalcitrant carbon matrix applied to soils), while the volatile gases are combusted for energy. This pathway requires no geological storage infrastructure and provides secondary agronomic or substitution value, but yields less energy than BES.

1.3 Spatial and Technoeconomic Architecture

Unlike traditional, site-specific TEAs, BiocharAG operates on a continuous spatial grid, recognizing that the optimal technology choice is geographically constrained.

The architecture relies on two primary analytical engines:

  1. Spatial Routing and Logistics Engine: Calculates the logistical constraints of each grid cell. For the BECCS pathway, the model routes captured \(CO_2\) to the nearest viable geological sink (accounting for onshore vs. offshore status) and calculates the exact distance. It applies a terrain tortuosity multiplier and assigns the appropriate transport modality (e.g., hub-and-spoke pipeline networks or maritime shipping).
  2. Thermodynamic and Economic Engine: Processes the physics and finances of the chosen plant capacity. It calculates the mass and energy balances for combustion and pyrolysis, accounts for capital expenditures (CAPEX), operational expenditures (OPEX), transport fees, injection costs, and revenue streams from electricity sales and carbon markets.

The final output is a series of spatial layers that map the most profitable technology for every specific location, providing decision-makers with a nuanced view of regional carbon management strategies.

2 Technoeconomic Pathways

2.1 1. Bioenergy Systems (BES)

BES represents a traditional biomass power plant. The primary value stream is electricity generation, offsetting fossil fuel emissions.

2.1.1 Assumptions:

  • Capital Cost: Sized around a reference $3,000/kW at 50 MW capacity.
  • O&M: Fixed percentage of Capital Cost (e.g., 4%).
  • Efficiency: 30% electrical efficiency.

2.2 2. Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS)

BECCS builds upon BES by adding a carbon capture unit and a transport network to geologic storage sinks.

2.2.1 Capture Penalty & Costs

BECCS comes with an efficiency penalty. The baseline electrical efficiency is reduced (e.g., from 30% to 28%) to account for the parasitic energy load of the capture process.

2.2.2 Transport & Storage Equations

The cost of transporting \(CO_2\) is broken into a hub-and-spoke pipeline model for onshore sinks and a shipping model for offshore/long-distance sinks.

  • Tortuosity Factor: Pipeline distances account for real-world routing limitations by multiplying the Euclidean distance by \(1.25\).
  • Mode Selection:
    • If distance > 1000 km (or 700 km for high volumes > 6 Mtpa) or if the sink is offshore, maritime shipping is selected.
    • Else, a pipeline network is constructed.

Pipeline Cost ($/Mg \(CO_2\)): \[ Cost_{pipe} = CAPEX_{annual} + OPEX_{annual} \] The CAPEX scales non-linearly using a diameter-based power law (typically \(scale\_factor = 0.6\)).

Dynamic Storage Cost: Injection costs adjust dynamically based on the sink type: * Onshore: ~$12/Mg \(CO_2\) * Offshore: ~$40/Mg \(CO_2\)

2.3 3. Bioenergy with Biochar Storage (BEBCS)

BEBCS utilizes pyrolysis/gasification to coproduce biochar and electricity.

2.3.1 Biomass Partitioning

  • Carbon Sequestration: \(C_{seq} = Yield_{bc} \times C\_content_{bc} \times F_{perm}\)
    • Where \(F_{perm}\) is the permanent stable fraction of biochar carbon in the soil, empirically derived from H:C ratios and soil temperature.

2.3.2 Biochar Agronomic Value

The economic value of biochar can be modeled dynamically through mechanistic substitution rather than static market prices: \[ Value_{ag} = Value_{lime} + Value_{nutrients} + Value_{physical} \]

3 Regional & Spatial Adjustments

3.1 Fuel Quality Penalty (Ash Content)

High-ash feedstocks (e.g., crop residues) suffer a penalty compared to clean wood chips due to slagging and boiler fouling. * Triggers higher CAPEX multipliers (requires Fluidized Bed vs Stoker). * Triggers higher O&M due to ash disposal and maintenance. * Lowers overall electrical efficiency.

3.2 Logistical Sourcing

Biomass transport (trucking) relies on the average transport distance, typically assumed as \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the collection radius, utilizing both fixed loading/unloading rates and variable per-kilometer rates.

4 Economic Core Equations

4.0.1 Annuity Factor

Capital investments are annualized over the project’s lifetime: \[ Annuity Factor = \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-t}}{r} \] Where \(r\) is the discount rate (e.g., 10%) and \(t\) is the lifetime (e.g., 20 or 30 years).

4.0.2 Net Present Value (NPV)

Each pathway resolves into a net value (\(/Mg Feedstock):\)$ Net_Value = Revenue (Electricity, Carbon, Agronomic) - Costs (CAPEX, OPEX, Transport, Storage) $$

4.1 CO2 Transport and Routing Logistics

The spatial routing of captured \(CO_2\) is governed by an economic optimization algorithm that dynamically selects the lowest-cost transport modality—pipeline or maritime shipping—for every grid cell. To account for terrain avoidance and right-of-way routing, all Euclidean distances are adjusted by a standard tortuosity multiplier of 1.25.

4.1.1 Maritime Shipping Modality

For offshore geological sinks, or for highly isolated onshore sources, \(CO_2\) is transported via maritime or rail networks. This modality is modeled with a step-cost function dominated by the capital-intensive fixed entry costs of liquefaction and terminal buffering, paired with highly efficient variable voyage costs:

\[C_{shipping} = C_{liquefaction} + C_{terminal} + (c_{voyage} \cdot d_{effective})\]

4.1.2 Hub-and-Spoke Pipeline Modality (Piecewise Linear)

Standard length-invariant TEA cost functions for \(CO_2\) pipelines (e.g., using a 0.6 capacity scaling factor) adequately model regional networks but fundamentally underestimate the capital and energetic burdens of long-distance transport. Specifically, maintaining \(CO_2\) in a dense/supercritical phase over extended distances requires the deployment of intermediate booster compression stations to overcome frictional pressure drop (McCoy & Rubin, 2008).

To model this without computationally intensive grid-level pump topology, pipeline CAPEX is calculated using a piecewise linear hub-and-spoke framework.

1. The Feeder Leg: Sources build a dedicated feeder pipeline for the first 50 km (\(d_{feeder}\)), bearing the full capacity-scaled CAPEX. 2. The Trunk Leg (Base Phase): For distances up to 700 km, the source connects to a regional trunkline operating at an efficient scale (3 Mtpa), paying only a proportional mass share of the CAPEX. 3. The Trunk Leg (Booster Phase): For distances exceeding 700 km, the marginal cost of the trunkline increases by a penalty factor (\(P_{booster} = 2.0\)) to approximate the compounding capital and operational costs of intermediate booster stations.

The trunkline CAPEX share (\(CAPEX_{trunk}\)) is calculated as:

\[ CAPEX_{trunk} = \begin{cases} C_{base} \cdot (d_{effective} - 50) & \text{if } d_{effective} \le 700 \\ C_{base} \cdot (700 - 50) + (C_{base} \cdot P_{booster}) \cdot (d_{effective} - 700) & \text{if } d_{effective} > 700 \end{cases} \]

Where \(C_{base}\) is the unit length cost of the efficient-scale trunkline, scaled to the user’s proportional mass.

4.1.3 Spatial Economic Optimization

To eliminate artifactual spatial boundaries, the model does not enforce rigid distance limits. For all onshore sinks, the algorithm evaluates both modalities and selects the optimal route:

\[Cost_{transport} = \min(C_{pipeline}, C_{shipping})\]

Because of the piecewise booster penalty, the \(C_{pipeline}\) curve steepens significantly beyond 700 km, guaranteeing a natural, continuous mathematical intersection with the flat \(C_{shipping}\) cost floor. This allows the model to smoothly transition to shipping modalities at distances consistent with the break-even thresholds identified by the Zero Emissions Platform (ZEP, 2011).

7 Carbon Intensity of Displaced Electricity

To robustly account for the climate mitigation benefit of bioenergy generation, we employed a consequential Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework. Rather than assuming new generation offsets the historical average grid mix—which often overestimates carbon savings by including legacy fossil baseload—we estimated the Marginal Carbon Intensity (MCI) of displaced energy. For each fixed 250 MWth plant archetype evaluated (encompassing Bioenergy, BECCS, and BEBCS configurations), the emissions offset was parameterized under two bounding scenarios: an empirical Business-as-Usual (BAU) baseline and a Paris-aligned counterfactual.

7.1 Empirical Baseline (Short-Run Marginal Capacity)

Under the BAU scenario, we assumed that new bioenergy capacity displaces the technologies currently actively expanding on the regional grid. We derived empirical MCIs by isolating generation sources that experienced positive generation growth (\(\Delta G > 0\)) over the five-year window from 2018 to 2023.

Historical electricity supply data were obtained from Ember. This dataset aggregates multi-country inventories, including the EIA, Eurostat, the Energy Institute, and the UN, alongside primary national sources such as China’s National Bureau of Statistics. The generation pathways mapped in this dataset were cross-referenced against standardized Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) lifecycle carbon intensities. The regional MCI was subsequently calculated as the generation-weighted average of these expanding sources:

\[MCI = \frac{\sum ( \Delta G_{i} \times CI_{i} )}{\sum \Delta G_{i}}\]

where \(\Delta G_{i}\) represents the net positive generation added for source \(i\) over the selected timeframe, and \(CI_{i}\) represents the static IPCC greenhouse gas intensity for that specific technology.

7.2 Paris-Aligned Counterfactual (Long-Run Structural Shift)

To account for structural shifts in energy systems operating under strict carbon budgets (e.g., \(1.5^\circ\text{C}\) or \(2.0^\circ\text{C}\) targets), we introduced a secondary counterfactual bounding scenario. In deep mitigation pathways, the deployment of carbon-negative technologies does not primarily displace high-emission fossil fuels, as these are inherently phased out by policy caps. Instead, integrated assessment modeling suggests that the introduction of baseload BECCS frequently displaces the most capital-intensive low-carbon alternatives. Under this sensitivity scenario, we assumed the displaced marginal generation is nuclear power, which reduces the grid displacement credit to a lifecycle equivalent of \(12\text{ gCO}_2\text{eq kWh}^{-1}\).
